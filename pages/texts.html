Texts

From 1959 onwards, Gustav Metzger published a series of manifestos that articulated his vision for auto-destructive and auto-creative art. These texts remain foundational documents in the history of contemporary art and political activism.

  • Auto-Destructive Art

    First Manifesto · London, 4 November 1959

    Metzger's first manifesto was distributed on a typed sheet on the occasion of his exhibition at 14 Monmouth Street, a coffee house run by the kinetic artist Brian Robins, held 9–30 November 1959. In it, he defined auto-destructive art as “primarily a form of public art for industrial societies” — a total unity of idea, site, form, colour, method, and timing of the disintegrative process.

    Excerpt

    Auto-destructive art is primarily a form of public art for industrial societies.

    Self-destructive painting, sculpture and construction is a total unity of idea, site, form, colour, method, and timing of the disintegrative process.

    Auto-destructive art can be created with natural forces, traditional art techniques and technological techniques.

    The amplified sound of the auto-destructive process can be an element of the total conception.

    The artist may collaborate with scientists, engineers.

    Self-destructive art can be machine produced and factory assembled.

    Auto-destructive paintings, sculptures and constructions have a life time varying from a few moments to twenty years. When the disintegrative process is complete the work is to be removed from the site and scrapped.

    Manuscript held in The Gustav Metzger Foundation Collection at Tate Archive, London.

  • Auto-Destructive Art

    Second Manifesto · London, 10 March 1960

    In the second version of the manifesto, Metzger expanded upon his vision, unequivocally stating that “Auto-destructive art is art which contains within itself an agent which automatically leads to its destruction within a period of time not to exceed twenty years.” The manifesto further articulated the relationship between auto-destructive art and the social and political conditions of the twentieth century.

    Excerpt

    Auto-destructive art is art which contains within itself an agent which automatically leads to its destruction within a period of time not to exceed twenty years.

    Other forms of auto-destructive art involve manual manipulation. There are forms of auto-destructive art where the artist has a tight control over the nature and timing of the disintegrative process, and there are other forms where the process is left to chance.

    Auto-destructive art is an attack on capitalist values and the drive to nuclear annihilation.

  • Auto-Destructive Art, Machine Art, Auto-Creative Art

    Third Manifesto (South Bank Manifesto) · London, 23 June 1961

    The third manifesto, also known as the South Bank Manifesto, introduced the concept of auto-creative art alongside auto-destructive art. It was published on the occasion of Metzger's first public demonstration of auto-destructive art on the South Bank, London, where he donned a gas mask and sprayed acid onto sheets of nylon. The manifesto includes reprints of the first two manifestos.

    Excerpt

    Each visible fact absolutely expresses its reality.

    Auto-destructive art is art which contains within itself an agent which automatically leads to its destruction within a period of time not to exceed twenty years. Other forms of auto-destructive art involve manual manipulation. There are forms of auto-destructive art where the artist has a tight control over the nature and timing of the disintegrative process, and there are other forms where the process is left to chance.

    Auto-destructive art is committed to a left-wing revolutionary position in politics, and to struggles against future wars.

    Auto-creative art is art of change, growth, movement.

  • Manifesto World

    Fourth Manifesto · London, 7 October 1962

    Metzger's fourth manifesto, ‘Manifesto World’, broadened the scope of his thinking beyond art into a comprehensive political and philosophical vision, addressing the global conditions of industrial society and the potential for annihilation.

  • On Random Activity in Material/Transforming Works of Art

    Fifth Manifesto · London, 30 July 1964

    Published in Signals: Newsbulletin of the Centre for Advanced Creative Study, this manifesto further developed Metzger's thinking on the relationship between chance, material processes, and artistic creation.

  • Art of Liquid Crystals

    London, January 1966

    Published by Better Books, London. A text exploring the artistic potential of liquid crystals, which became a central medium in Metzger's work, notably in the Liquid Crystal Environment (1965/2005).

  • Zagreb Manifesto

    1969 · with Jonathan Benthall and Gordon Hyde

    Published in Studio International, London, June 1969. A collaborative manifesto addressing the intersection of art, technology, and social responsibility.

  • Nature Demised Resurrects as Environment

    1992

    An essay reflecting Metzger's deep engagement with environmental issues and the relationship between nature, destruction, and artistic practice. Published in Damaged Nature, Auto-Destructive Art (Coracle, 1996).

  • Remember Nature

    2013

    A retrospective manifesto calling for a renewed engagement with nature and environmental consciousness. This became the basis for a nationwide day of action in 2015, marked by a conference at the Serpentine Gallery, London.
