Gustav Metzger

Portrait of Gustav Metzger

Gustav Metzger. © The Gustav Metzger Foundation

Gustav Metzger was born in Nuremberg, Germany on 10 April 1926 to Polish-Jewish parents. He arrived in Britain on the Kindertransport in 1939 with his older brother. Most of their immediate family were killed in the Holocaust.

From 1945 to 1953, Metzger studied art in Cambridge, London, Antwerp, and Oxford. For much of this time he was associated with the artist David Bomberg, who proved to be a formative influence.

Political Activism

By 1958, Metzger had become heavily involved in anti-capitalist, anti-consumerist movements and the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament. In 1960, he was a founder member of the Committee of 100, along with the philosopher Bertrand Russell. This affiliation led to a brief imprisonment in 1961 with Russell and other members of the Committee for encouraging mass non-violent civil disobedience.

Auto-Destructive Art

Metzger's political activism provided the foundation for his first artist manifesto in 1959, titled ‘Auto-Destructive Art’, which he described “as a desperate last-minute subversive political weapon.”

Auto-destructive art — a public art form — sought to provide a mirror of a social and political system that Metzger felt was progressing towards total obliteration. At the heart of his practice, which spanned over 65 years, are a series of constantly opposing yet interdependent forces, such as destruction and creation.

Acid Action Painting, South Bank, London, 1961

Gustav Metzger, Recreation of First Public Demonstration of Auto-Destructive Art, South Bank, London, 1961. © The Gustav Metzger Foundation

That year he also created his first auto-destructive artworks by spraying acid onto sheets of nylon, which caused the disintegration of the work itself, as another form of anti-nuclear protest. Three nylon canvases coloured white, black, and red were arranged behind each other; acid was painted, flung, and sprayed onto the nylon which corroded at the point of contact within fifteen seconds.

Auto-destructive art is primarily a form of public art for industrial societies. Self-destructive painting, sculpture and construction is a total unity of idea, site, form, colour, method, and timing of the disintegrative process. — Gustav Metzger, Auto-Destructive Art Manifesto, 1959

Destruction in Art Symposium

Together with John Sharkey, Metzger organised the seminal Destruction in Art Symposium in London in 1966, bringing together artists, scientists, and thinkers from around the world to explore the creative and destructive dimensions of art.

Guitarist Pete Townshend from The Who studied with Metzger at Ealing School of Art, and during the 1960s, Metzger's work was projected on screens at The Who concerts.

Art Strike

In 1974, Metzger called for artists to support a three-year Art Strike (1977–1980), and he did, in fact, withdraw from the art world during those years. This call is further evidence of his sustained position against the commodification of art and the capitalist art market.

Later Work and Legacy

Since the mid-2000s, Metzger's legacy was rediscovered in a string of major institutional exhibitions, including the Serpentine Gallery, London (2009); the New Museum, New York (2011–2012); Tate Britain, London (2016); and posthumously at Hauser & Wirth Somerset (2021) and Hauser & Wirth Los Angeles (2024).

Gustav Metzger lived and worked in London, where he died on 1 March 2017 at the age of 90.
